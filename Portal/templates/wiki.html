General Online Safety Tips

Never give out personal information: If a company reaches out to you, never give them your personal information. Only ever if you reached out to them, and double checked the credientials of the website or phone number called.

Look for “Urgency” in messages trying to get you to click: Many phishing scams try and scare a user. They want you to panic and click their links and give them all your personal information. Real, reliable companies do not use scare tactics in their emails.

Never share your 2FA codes: No company will call or ask you for your 2 Factor Authentication codes ever. It is likely a scammer trying to access your account and bypass your security and log into your account.

Don't login to sensetive accounts while on Public Wifi: Hackers can monitor and wait on public networks for you to access sites such as banks, and then they can obtain your username and password or card details from logging in.

Keep your software Up to Date: Keeping your software Up to Date on your phone and computer can help ensure safety from security patches issued by the manufacturer. These patches help make it harder for hackers to crack.

Popular Scams

AI Scams

AI scams are becoming far more common in todays society. Many people are falling for AI fakes. A common example is a voice scam, where the voice sounds like it is from a loved one, and they target their parents or grandparents. This makes many panic and send money to these people. How to prevent: Hang up and call that family member they are impersonating, or create a Family "Safe" Word.

QR Codes

Scammers will place fake QR codes around various areas such as restraunts or even in emails. If someone scans one and they may look like a legit site, but they can steal all of your information. Always double check a QR code link before you click on it.

Tech Support/You have a Virus

A Popup will show up on your screen looking like its from a legit security company. These can freeze your screen, causing many users to panic and calling the help number on the popup. Upon calling, they will ask for remote access and then can gain full access to your computer. With this they can steal all of your information by tricking you into panicking.

Explanations

IP Address instead of Domain

What: Address bar shows numbers instead of a URL name.

Why Bad: Malicious websites may try and hide identity and not have a registered domain name.

Url contains @ symbol

What: The URL has an @ symbol.

Why Bad: Scammers try and hide parts of URL and create a misleading link with an @ symbol.

URL subdomain impersonates known brand

What: A popular brand is used in the subdomain.

Why Bad: Scammers try and trick users and gaining their trust thinking they are clicking on the actual site.

Domain uses internationalized charcters

What: The domain may contain characters from other languages but look like English.

Why Bad: This looks like a legitimate site, but it actually could be a malicious link pretending to be the real thing.

Login form submits credentials to a different domain

What: Sends your username and password to another domain than the one you are on.

Why Bad: This sends your information to another domain which is most likely stealing that information and looking to gain access to your accounts.

Domain uses a TLD commonly associated with phishing

What: Unlike a .org, this site ends in a most common phishing way, like .xyz or .top that are cheap, disposable sites.

Why Bad: Cheap domains that can be bought and used for large scale phishing attacks to gain ones information.

URL contains a non-standard port number

What: The URL contains a specific port numbers.

Why Bad: Most web uses standard port numbers, but the non-standard ports could show a server running on a hijacked service.

URL has excessive subdomains

What: The URL contains many parts and sections to it.

Why Bad: Create long, hidden address inside of a domain that hides the actual suspicious domain so you can’t notice it easily.

Domain contains many hyphens

What: The domain contains many hyphens/dashes.

Why Bad: Many legitimate sites don’t have hyphens, while scammers will create a name with hyphens that looks like the real companies website.

Site does not use HTTPS

What: The site uses HTTP:// instead of the secure HTTPS://.

Why Bad: The site is not encrypted. Many new phishing sites skip this to just be able to keep pushing out more websites to scam with.

Unusually long URL

What: The URL contains many characters.

Why Bad: This is used to hide malicious codes, redirects, and other suspicious things from the view of the user.

URL uses a link shortening service

What: The URL uses a service to shorten their link.

Why Bad: This makes it so the destination is masked, so the user is unable to see where the link goes before clicking on it.

URL contains excessive percent-encoding

What: The URL has things such as %20 or other codes in them.

Why Bad: This is used to confuse and jumble the URL so the user can’t easily detect its bad.

Page contains a login form

What: The page is asking you to login with some username/email and password.

Why Bad: Not bad by itself, but be cautious whenever typing in sensitive information on a website.

Page favicon loaded from external domain

What: The small icon on the browser tab is pulled from somewhere else.

Why Bad: Scammers can “borrow” the brands real icon they are trying to impersonate to make their site look more legitimate.

Page contaisn hidden iframes

What: The page has invisible “windows” on the site.

Why Bad: Can load malicious content and have the user unknowingly click on things in the background without their permission.

Page disables right-click (prevents inspection)

What: The site blocks you from opening the inspection, right-click menu.

Why Bad: This is a way to stop people from seeing what is going on behind the scenes like where your data is being sent to. They are trying to hide information from you.

Many non-functional links of page

What: Links on the site go no where, such as clicking on an About Us link.

Why Bad: A lot of scammers only worry about the main functionally of the home page to steal information, so they don’t bother filling in all the other buttons that are just there for show to look mor legitimate.

Thin page with login form (minimal content)

What: Basically nothing on the page except for a login box.

Why Bad: Used for data harvesting because real websites would usually have some sort of navigation even without needing to login.

Page uses auto-redirect

What: The page auto-redirects you to another site.

Why Bad: Used to send users to other sites and have hidden links to hide origin of the phishing/scam site.

Most page resources loaded from external domains

What: Almost all of the images and scripts are being loaded from another site.

Why Bad: A site looking and pretending to be the real company by having it link directly from the legitimate site.

Prevention

  • Create strong passwords atleast 12 characters more. Use numbers, special characters, and not things such as name or birthday.
  • Never share your password with anyone. Passwords should be confidential and only you know them.
  • Do not use the same password for every login.
  • Use a Password Manager to keep track of your passwords so you don't lose them and this keeps them secure.
  • Always double check the Full URL before clicking.
  • Never click direclty on links sent from suspicious email addresses.
  • Hover before clicking on a link to see the full address.
  • Don't allow location or microphone services on your mobile phone.
